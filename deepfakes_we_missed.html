The Deepfakes We Missed

We Built Detectors for a Threat That Didn't Arrive
Shaina Raza
Vector Institute for Artificial Intelligence
Paper arXiv Code Slides
Abstract

Nearly a decade of ML research on deepfake detection has been organized around a threat model inherited from 2017–2019: face-swap and talking-head manipulation of public figures, motivated by concerns about large-scale misinformation and video-evidence fraud. This position paper argues that the threat the field prepared for did not arrive, and the threats that did arrive are substantially different. An accounting of deepfake incidents in 2022–2026 shows that the dominant observed harms are peer-generated non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII), voice-clone scam calls targeting families and finance workers, and emotional-manipulation fraud. The predicted large-scale public-figure deepfake catastrophe did not materialize during the 2024 global election cycle despite extensive preparation. Meanwhile, research effort, benchmarks, and detection methods remain concentrated on the inherited threat model. We argue this misalignment is now the dominant bottleneck on real-world deepfake defense, not model capability, and support this position with empirical accounting of research effort and harm distribution, a diagnosis of structural persistence mechanisms, and three concrete technical research agendas for the under-defended harm categories.

Position. The dominant bottleneck on real-world deepfake defense is no longer model capability but a misalignment between the threat model the field organized around and the harms that actually materialized at scale. The ML community should substantially rebalance toward: real-time voice-clone detection in telecommunications, on-device privacy-preserving NCII detection with victim-centered workflows, and messaging-layer defenses for peer-distributed synthetic content.

Contributions
  1. A 438-paper classification of detection research (2017–2025) across a five-category threat taxonomy (T1–T5), showing 71.0% concentration on T1 public-figure video.
  2. A harm distribution synthesized from IC3, IWF, AIID, StopNCII.org, and victim surveys, concentrated on NCII, voice-clone scams, and peer-distributed manipulation.
  3. A structural diagnosis of why the misalignment persists: benchmark inheritance, dataset-ethics asymmetry, and salience-driven attention allocation.
  4. Three concrete research agendas for the under-defended categories, defended on harm-proportionality and ML-tractability.
Proposed Research Agendas
I. Real-Time Voice-Clone Detection for Telecommunications
Streaming synthesis-probability estimation under telephony codecs with sub-second latency budgets, channel-robust evaluation under realistic packet loss and bandwidth restriction, and speaker-conditional verification layered on synthesis detection.
II. On-Device Privacy-Preserving NCII Detection
Low-reference few-shot verification within victim trust boundaries, federated and on-device inference where candidate images never leave the device, and workflow integration with StopNCII.org hash matching, platform trust-and-safety APIs, and national hotlines.
III. Messaging-Layer Defenses for Peer-Distributed Content
Tiny on-device detectors operating before encryption under constrained compute, re-encoding-robust provenance signals that survive messaging-platform pipelines, and graph-based distribution-pattern signals under privacy-preserving constraints.
